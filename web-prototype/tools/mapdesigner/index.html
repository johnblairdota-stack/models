What am I looking at

This is one procedurally generated house, drawn as a floor plan, seen from above. Nothing here is hand-drawn — a seed number goes in, this comes out. Press → to see the next one.

The generator places the six estate rooms free-form, flush against each other, then fills every scrap of space left over with corridors so that no wall in the house has empty nothing on the other side of it. That is the whole idea: a wall with a room on both sides is a wall you can dig through.

■ Orange boundaries are diggable — you can smash through them. The little bright blocks along them are the actual dig panels the game would build, at the real 2.56 / 2.96 / 5.72 m lengths it already ships; the ■ brown between and around them is the same wall, just the bit no panel fitted on. ■ Cyan boundaries cannot be dug at all — under 1.20 m of shared wall, so there is nowhere to put a dig face. Cyan on this plan means exactly one thing: a hard stop.

And there is one bright lime blob on every diggable wall. That is the soft spot — the one place on that wall where a dig gets you through. Everywhere else on an orange wall you dig all the way and hit brick you cannot break, so orange means "somewhere worth searching", not "a way out of this room". Find a soft spot and that brick switches off for the whole house, for everybody. You cannot see it in the game — this plan shows it so you can see how much wall a player has to chew through to find one.

⚠️ The unbreakable brick itself is NOT on this plan, and it never was. ⟵ you asked about this, and the answer you got before was wrong. It stands behind the orange, and this is a view from above, so there is nowhere to draw it. What you can see is the one gap in it, and that gap is the lime blob. It used to be drawn cyan, which made it look like the hard stops it is the opposite of — that is what you were seeing, and it was a real fault in this tool, not a misunderstanding.

The house you play now has 7 hand-authored dig edges, 14 panels, 47.7 m — which is 52.6% of the wall it actually has between rooms. A generated house gets 92.8%, on four times as much wall. That is the number this whole thing is for.

Corridors are the blue-grey shapes with the dotted fill. In the game they are dark — that is the point of them, and eventually they are where the hunter lives — but on a plan they are drawn light so you can see the circulation. Solid black hatched blocks are void: leftover space too small to walk in, filled with masonry. Void is a failure and it is counted.

Everything you see is drawn from harness/genspike.mjs, the same file that produced the 512-seed measurements. This tool has no generator of its own — if a number here disagrees with the harness, the tool is wrong, and the VERIFICATION panel at the bottom checks exactly that on every single frame.

Is this a good house? ← the important bit

Nobody can teach the generator what a good house looks like except you. The fix for flat, boring plans is a scoring term, and a scoring term needs examples — your examples. One click per plan. It writes to tools/mapdesigner/labels.jsonl in the project, so the next agent can actually use it.

Judge it as a place to be chased through, not as a drawing. Would you know where you were? Is there anywhere to go that is not just forward? After a verdict it jumps to the next seed automatically.

Legend — every colour, in the player's terms

The three doors, and one of them was drawn wrong

You said the red chained doors should be the ones the hunter attacks. They are the opposite, and the colour was teaching you that. Fixed on the plan; here is what the code actually says, checked on disk rather than remembered.

on the planwhat it is to play
green gap OPEN. A hole. You walk through, he walks through, it costs nothing and it is the route the house gives you.
amber + magenta chevrons BREACHABLE — this is the one he batters. Six blows, about fourteen seconds of banging, and he is through. You can break it too, and cheaply. The chain you see on half of them in-game is dressing, seeded per doorway and never read off what the door actually is — that is deliberate, so counting padlocks tells you nothing.
black + red cross CHAINED — nobody forces it. Not your hammer, not the nail gun, not him. The code marks it damageable: false and the hunter's own door list skips it. The way past a chained door is the wall next to it.
arrow off the edge THE WAY OUT. A 15–25 s siege with a yard behind it. In the house you play there are fourteen of these sites and only one is live per run; the other thirteen get padlocked and are the decoys. The generator only places the live one — it does not model the decoys yet, and that is a gap, not a design.

Where the confusion came from, since it is worth knowing: the function in the game that hands the hunter his list of doors to hammer is called chainedDoorsOf() — and the first thing it does is throw out every chained door. The name is a lie in src/. This tool now draws the set, not the name.

Is it flat?

The worst failure mode, and no summary number caught it before. A flat plan is one file of rooms with nothing behind anything — no upstairs, no round-the-back. They score well on everything else and they are the dullest houses the generator makes. 42.4% of seeds are flat.

Each slice of the bar is a step along the house's long axis, coloured by how many rooms you would walk through crossing it. ■ 1 ■ 2 ■ 3+

The test is min(rooms crossed left-to-right, rooms crossed top-to-bottom), and 2 or less is flat. It is checked against the one house we know is good: the mansion you already play scores 3, so any rule that called it flat would be a broken rule. That control is asserted in the harness's own self-test.

The zero-dig pocket — i.e. how much house you get for free

Shaded yellow on the plan. It is the part of the house you can walk to from where you spawn without swinging the hammer once — open doors only, nothing forced, nothing dug. That is the whole of it.

It is drawn because you cannot see it otherwise: a padlocked door and a wall you have to dig are the same line on a floor plan and completely different to play. A big pocket is a house that lets you in and the run opens with exploring. A tiny one is a house that is a wall from the first second. Neither is automatically right — that is exactly why it is worth looking at.

🧱 Slabs — one wall, not tiles

Turn on SLAB ARM in the Show list. Each diggable wall is redrawn as the ■ violet faces it would actually become, with a ■ red tick at every seam.

The cap is 35 m and there is no minimum. Re-measured on this tree (node harness/_slab1-cost.mjs): a 34.80 m face costs 1.077 ms of the 2.00 ms CPU budget, an 85.90 m face costs 2.576 ms and blows it. The straight-line fit crosses the budget at about 66 m — but nobody has measured between 35 and 86, so the cap sits at the longest length actually measured good.

⚠️ And the cap never fires here. Over 512 seeds the longest single stretch of diggable wall this generator makes is 31.33 m (p95 27.20 — the gallery's own long side). The "85.90 m" quoted elsewhere is the total of every separate stretch two rooms share, not a wall; no face that long exists. So the cap is a guard rail, and the thing actually cutting walls in half is doors.

🚨 Not solved, and not pretended otherwise: a slab cannot contain a doorway. Every wall with a door in it therefore comes out as two faces with a seam between them — which is the one thing you said you did not want. Either the wall machine learns to punch a hole in a face, or generated walls get seams at every door. That is a decision, not a bug, and it is yours.

Do corridors cost contact?

No — they nearly double it. This is the same seed built twice: once as you have it, once with gap 0 (rooms packed flush, no corridors at all).

Packing rooms tight against each other is the intuitive reading of "maximum surface area contact" and it is the worse one. room|room is one boundary; room|corridor|room is two.

Difficulty — the slot, not the score

You asked for difficulty estimation "when the hunter is better developed". He is not, so there is deliberately no score here — a number invented now would be a guess that later gets quoted as a measurement. What is here is every property a difficulty model will need, measured. When the hunter exists, the score is a weighted sum over exactly these rows and nothing else has to move.

The room library — and why it is the real limit

Five shapes. That is the whole library. study_w and study_e in spaces.js are byte-identical — 11.60 × 15.40 both — so the house has six spaces but five distinct footprints.

The arithmetic says roughly 10¹⁵ different arrangements. It does not matter. Every run the player walks into the same gallery, the same ballroom, the same two studies. They will run out of new-looking houses in about ten runs and the maths will be nowhere near exhausted. The library is the limit, not the generator — so a sixth footprint, or dressing the two studies differently, buys more than any dial on this page does.

      What the game would have to change to eat one
      
Short version, biggest job first — and none of it is
      done, deliberately.

      
1. The dressing has to stop being written in world coordinates. The
      gallery's entry in spaces.js is about 150 lines and only one of them is the
      footprint; the rest is lights, pilasters and order, and the light positions are absolute
      — the gallery's key light is at [11.6, 4.05, -30.55], which is only true where
      the gallery currently sits. Every one of those has to become room-local and get moved by the
      plan. This is the whole cost of the feature. Everything else on this list is small.

      
2. EXIT_SITES has to stop being a constant. It is computed
      at import today, and in a generated house the exit pool is different for every seed by
      construction. Which also means: every "seed s4 exits at…" note anywhere becomes
      meaningless in generated mode, so this must ship as a new mode next to the authored
      house, not as a replacement for it.

      
3. Dig ids become a version handshake. Two players on the same seed and
      different builds would derive different wall ids and desync silently. The export keys
      every boundary on a hash of the wall itself rather than its position in a list, which helps,
      but the generator version still has to be checked when a session starts.

      
4. Three times the dig panels. 46 spans per plan against the shipped
      14. The panel types stay at three, so the draw-call gate holds, but nobody has
      measured the renderer at 3× the panel count.

      
What comes free: the pathfinding already works this way, and the connector
      system generalises untouched.
